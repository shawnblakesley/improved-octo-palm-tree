# Week 27 — Worldbuilding, Character, and Theme

## Why This Matters

You know the difference between a system's internal data model and its public API. Worldbuilding is the data model of a fiction — mostly hidden, load-bearing, and dangerous when it contradicts what the surface exposes. Theme is the invariant your whole artifact should satisfy. And character is the interface the player actually touches. This week is about building a fictional world that stays internally consistent, giving it characters who change, and deciding what argument the whole thing is quietly making.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish worldbuilding, lore, and story and explain what each contributes to play.
- Articulate a game's theme as an argument and locate where its mechanics support or undercut it.
- Explain how tone is established and maintained across art, audio, systems, and text.
- Analyze a character arc and describe how mechanics can embody it.
- Draft a narrative design document section covering setting, tone, and consistency rules.

## Lesson

### Worldbuilding: the iceberg and the surface

**Worldbuilding** is the construction of a coherent fictional world: its history, cultures, economies, religions, and physical logic. **Setting** is the specific time, place, and social context in which the story occurs — the slice of the world the player actually inhabits. A well-built world gives every department a stable reference. Level designers know what a settlement should look like; writers know what a character would plausibly believe; audio knows what a place should sound like. This is the schema behind the fiction.

Most of that world is never shown. **Lore** is the accumulated background knowledge that gives the world depth but is rarely dramatized. The design principle from Pascal's famous apology applies exactly: the world should have depth that the player senses but does not fully see. *Dark Souls* is the exemplar. Its lore — the fate of the gods, the decline of kingdoms — is delivered through item descriptions, architecture, and fragments, so the world feels far larger than the story. *Hollow Knight* does the same for a fallen insect kingdom. The player never receives a history lecture; they assemble one, and that assembly is itself part of the experience.

Worldbuilding is not free, and more is not better. Every rule you invent becomes a constraint on future content and a promise to the player. A world with three consistent, load-bearing rules — say, *Fallout*'s retro-futurist technology, scarcity, and persistent radiation — does more work than a hundred decorative facts. The engineering instinct applies: build the smallest model that supports the content you need, then extend it deliberately, because every addition to the schema has downstream costs in art, level design, and writing.

### Lore vs. story

Keep three layers distinct: worldbuilding is the model, **lore** is the stored background, and **story** is the dramatized events. The failure mode is dumping the model onto the surface. A game that stops to explain its cosmology in the critical path has confused reference material with narrative. The better pattern is *selective delivery*: the deep construction exists for consistency, while only the events and details that serve the current beat reach the player. Optional, discoverable lore — codex entries, environmental detail, missable conversations — lets motivated players go deeper without taxing everyone else. This is progressive disclosure applied to fiction.

### Theme as argument

**Theme** is the central argument a work makes about human experience. The crucial word is *argument*, because a theme is not a topic ("love") but a position ("love is a form of selfishness that must be chosen anyway"). The best way to test whether a game has a theme is to ask what it rewards, punishes, repeats, and resolves — not what its dialogue says.

*The Last of Us* has a plot about smuggling a girl across a post-apocalyptic America. Its theme is about the selfishness of love: Joel's affection for Ellie ultimately overrides the possibility of a cure, and the game refuses to condemn or celebrate him. Every system — scarce resources, the brutality of combat, the repeated rescue and separation beats — supports that argument. *BioShock* argues about free will and objectivism and makes the player physically enact compliance. *Disco Elysium* argues about how ideology and memory constitute a person, and its skill system is the argument made playable.

When a game's mechanics support its theme, the result is **ludonarrative consonance**, the aligned counterpart to the dissonance from Week 25. When they contradict it, the mechanics usually win in the player's memory, because they are experienced rather than told.

Themes are usually discovered rather than assigned. A team often sets out to make a game about one thing — freedom, survival, revenge — and only during development notices what its systems are actually arguing. That is normal and healthy. The designer's job is to recognize the emerging argument and then make the whole artifact commit to it, cutting or reworking content that pulls in another direction. A theme that is only a marketing sentence has no effect; a theme that shapes what gets built has all of it.

### Tone

**Tone** is the prevailing emotional character of the experience — grim, melancholy, whimsical, wry, dread-soaked. Tone is not a writing decision; it is a cross-disciplinary constraint. Art direction, audio, color, animation weight, menu sound, and even loading times contribute. *Silent Hill 2* achieves its tone through fog, static, sound design, and deliberately awkward animation as much as through its script. *Journey* establishes awe through sand, light, and music. *Hades* sustains a wry, mythic tone across death, combat, and even its failure screens, so that losing feels like part of the voice rather than a rupture. A game can hold multiple tones, but transitions must be motivated; a whimsical cutscene in a dread-soaked horror game reads as a bug, not a joke, unless the design makes the shift mean something.

### Characterization and character arcs

**Characterization** is the construction of a character's traits, voice, desires, and contradictions. **Character arc** is the trajectory of change across the story, usually driven by the tension between a *want* (what the character pursues), a *flaw* (what is wrong with them), and a *need* (what they actually require to be whole). The want and the need usually conflict. *God of War* (2018) gives Kratos a want — to protect and raise his son — a flaw — a lifetime of rage and emotional withdrawal — and a need — to let himself be known. That arc is carried not only in scenes but in mechanics: the game's slower combat, the boy's growing independence, and the restraint the player must exercise all embody the change. In *Red Dead Redemption 2*, Arthur's arc runs through the honor system, letting the player's moment-to-moment behavior participate in who he becomes.

Games have an advantage film does not: the player can be implicated in the arc. When *Mass Effect* lets you shape Garrus's attitude across three games, the character's change is partly your doing, which makes his growth feel earned rather than observed. *Disco Elysium* goes further, making the protagonist's identity a negotiation among competing skills, so characterization and mechanics are the same system. The design question is always which parts of a character the player should watch and which they should author.

### Subtext, symbolism, and consistency

**Subtext** is meaning conveyed beneath the literal content: what a character will not say, what an interaction implies. It matters more in games than in film because the player is doing the interpretive work, and active inference creates stronger attachment than exposition. **Symbolism** — recurring objects, images, motifs — lets a designer carry meaning without dialogue: the fog and monsters of *Silent Hill 2* are not decoration but thematic statements, and its "Maria" character is a symbol the player must decode.

Holding all of this together requires **consistency**. Every world rule you establish is a promise; breaking it damages trust as surely as a physics bug. This is why teams keep a **narrative design document** — a living reference specifying setting, tone, characters, world rules, story structure, and delivery plan. It is the narrative equivalent of an interface contract: it lets writers, level designers, artists, and audio make locally independent decisions that remain mutually compatible. When two disciplines disagree about what the world allows, the document is what prevents the contradiction from shipping.

## Key Takeaways

- Worldbuilding is the deep model, lore is the stored background, and story is the dramatized surface; deliver selectively.
- Theme is an argument the whole game makes, tested by what it rewards, punishes, repeats, and resolves — not by what it says.
- Tone is a cross-disciplinary constraint carried by systems and sensation as much as by text.
- Character arcs arise from the conflict between want, flaw, and need, and mechanics can embody that change.
- Subtext and symbolism let the player infer meaning, which typically lands harder than explicit statement.
- Consistency is a promise to the player; the narrative design document is the contract that keeps disciplines aligned.

## Exercises

1. **Theme audit (hands-on).** Choose a game and write three candidate sentences of the form "This game argues that ___." For the one you find most defensible, list three systems or mechanics that support it and one that undercuts it. Use the undercutting element to write a one-sentence design note.
2. **Worldbuilding depth test.** Sketch a setting in ten facts, but decide which two reach the critical path as story, which three appear as optional lore, and which five remain background for the team only. Explain why each fact is placed where it is.
3. **Arc through mechanics.** Design a short character arc and specify one mechanic whose behavior changes as the arc progresses — such as a combat option that becomes unavailable, or a dialogue skill that unlocks. Write the want, flaw, and need in one sentence each, and describe the mechanic's change at the start, middle, and end.
